Belief propagation on trees

Department of Biostatistics and Computational Biology, University of Rochester Medical Center

Where we left off

Forward-backward turned a sum over \(K^T\) state paths into \(O(TK^2)\) work by pushing each sum as far inward as it would go.

  • What that used: the joint factorized into terms that each touch two neighbouring variables; every partial sum could be computed once and reused.
  • Nothing in the argument was about time. The chain is just the simplest arrangement with that property.
  • The next simplest is a tree, and the same two passes work there. The algorithm is called belief propagation.

In Lab 4 you built the data structure, wrote the two traversals, and simulated an alignment down a phylogeny. Today we hang the inference on that code, and then take the oldest tree model in biology seriously.

Goals

  • State what a tree structured model assumes, and why that assumption lets sums be pushed inward.
  • Derive the message rule, and the collect and distribute passes, by pushing sums inward on a small tree.
  • Implement belief propagation on the Node class and verify it against brute force and against forward-backward.
  • Recognize Felsenstein’s pruning algorithm as the collect pass, use it to score phylogenies, and use the distribute pass to infer ancestral states.

Tree structured models

Trees

An undirected graph is a tree if it is connected and has no cycles. Equivalently:

  • There is exactly one path between any two nodes.
  • \(n\) nodes, \(n - 1\) edges.
  • Removing any edge splits the graph into two pieces.

A tree structured model is a joint distribution whose factors each involve one node or one edge of some tree. The HMM is one: its factors are \(p(z_t \mid z_{t-1})\) on the edges and \(p(y_t \mid z_t)\) at the nodes, and the tree is a line.

A latent tree model

Latent variables at the internal nodes, observations at the leaves. The HMM had one observation hanging off each latent variable; here the shape is richer, but the factorization is the same kind of object.

Exercise. Write down the joint distribution \(p(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y})\) of this model by following the arrows: one factor per node, conditioned on whatever points at it. How many terms does the likelihood \(p(\mathbf{y})\) sum over if each \(z\) takes \(K\) values?

The joint distribution

Write \(\mathbf{z} = (z_1, \ldots, z_n)\) for the latent variables at the \(n\) nodes, one multivariate variable, and \(\mathbf{y}\) for the observations. For a directed tree with root \(r\), the chain rule along the arrows gives

\[ p(\mathbf{z}) = p(z_r) \prod_{v \neq r} p\big(z_v \mid z_{\text{pa}(v)}\big). \]

Each observation hangs off one node, so the joint with the data is

\[ p(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y}) = p(z_r) \prod_{v \neq r} p\big(z_v \mid z_{\text{pa}(v)}\big) \prod_{v} p(y_v \mid z_v), \]

where \(p(y_v \mid z_v) \equiv 1\) at a node with nothing observed. Each factor touches one node or one edge, and that is all the algorithm will use.

The assumption that does the work

Let \(v\) be a node and let \(T_1, T_2\) be two subtrees hanging off it. In a tree structured model,

\[ p(z_{T_1}, z_{T_2} \mid z_v) = p(z_{T_1} \mid z_v) \; p(z_{T_2} \mid z_v). \]

  • No factor touches both \(T_1\) and \(T_2\): every factor is a node or an edge, and the only route between the two subtrees runs through \(v\). Fix \(z_v\) and the joint splits into a piece on each side.
  • So a sum over \(T_1\) and a sum over \(T_2\) can be done separately and multiplied. That is the conditional independence we used on the chain, where \(z_t\) separated the past from the future.
  • With a cycle there would be two routes between some pair of variables, one node would not separate them, and the sums would not split.

The tree records this structure so we can read it off. Everything that follows is bookkeeping on top of this one fact.

The same problem as before

The likelihood of the leaves sums the joint over every latent node:

\[ p(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{z_1} \cdots \sum_{z_n} p(z_r) \prod_{v \neq r} p\big(z_v \mid z_{\text{pa}(v)}\big) \prod_{v} p(y_v \mid z_v). \]

\(K^n\) terms, where \(n\) is now the number of internal nodes rather than the length of a sequence.

Same disease, same cure. Push the sums inward.

Trees are not an exotic special case

  • Phylogenies and clonal trees, from the lab: species, viral lineages, B cell receptors, tumour subclones. Ancestors at the internal nodes, sequenced data at the leaves. The second half of today.
  • Hierarchical models. Patients within clinics within regions. The latent variables are the group level parameters, and observing one clinic tells you about the others only through their shared parent. Same shape, no evolution.
  • Hierarchical labels. A taxonomy of diagnoses or cell types, where a label at a leaf implies every ancestor label.
  • Pedigrees. Almost a tree, and next week we see why “almost” matters.

Pushing sums inward

A small tree, written out

Four leaves, three latent nodes, states in \(\{1, \dots, K\}\). Nothing is observed at the latent nodes themselves.

\[ p(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{z_0} \sum_{z_1} \sum_{z_2} p(z_0) \, p(z_1 \mid z_0) \, p(z_2 \mid z_0) \, p(y_3 \mid z_1) \, p(y_4 \mid z_1) \, p(y_5 \mid z_2) \, p(y_6 \mid z_2) \]

\(z_1\) appears in three factors, \(z_2\) in three others, and none of the six involves both. Push each sum through everything it does not touch:

\[ p(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{z_0} p(z_0) \underbrace{\Big[ \sum_{z_1} p(z_1 \mid z_0) \, p(y_3 \mid z_1) \, p(y_4 \mid z_1) \Big]}_{m_{1 \to 0}(z_0)} \underbrace{\Big[ \sum_{z_2} p(z_2 \mid z_0) \, p(y_5 \mid z_2) \, p(y_6 \mid z_2) \Big]}_{m_{2 \to 0}(z_0)}. \]

Each bracket depends only on \(z_0\) and only on the subtree below one child. Three sums of \(K^2\) terms instead of one sum of \(K^3\).

A quantity for every subtree

Inside the first bracket, \(p(y_3 \mid z_1)\) is itself a function of \(z_1\) alone, “what leaf 3 says about \(z_1\)”. Call it \(m_{3 \to 1}(z_1)\). Then the bracket is

\[ m_{1 \to 0}(z_0) = \sum_{z_1} p(z_1 \mid z_0) \, m_{3 \to 1}(z_1) \, m_{4 \to 1}(z_1), \]

the same shape one level up. Define, for every non-root node \(v\) with parent \(u\),

\[ \boxed{\; m_{v \to u}(z_u) = \sum_{z_v} p(z_v \mid z_u) \, p(y_v \mid z_v) \prod_{c \,\in\, \text{children}(v)} m_{c \to v}(z_v) \;} \]

  • \(m_{v \to u}\) is a vector over the states of the parent: everything in the subtree at \(v\), summarized for \(u\).
  • Every message needs its children’s messages first: post-order.

What a message is

Write \(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor v \rfloor}\) for the observations in the subtree rooted at \(v\), \(v\) included.

\[ m_{v \to u}(z_u) = p(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor v \rfloor} \mid z_u), \qquad p(y_v \mid z_v) \prod_{c \,\in\, \text{children}(v)} m_{c \to v}(z_v) = p(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor v \rfloor} \mid z_v). \]

  • A message is a likelihood: what the data below \(v\) say about the state at the far end of the edge. On the small tree, \(m_{1 \to 0}(z_0) = p(y_3, y_4 \mid z_0)\).
  • The product inside the rule is the same likelihood one level down, conditioned on \(z_v\); the sum over \(z_v\) against \(p(z_v \mid z_u)\) moves the conditioning up to \(z_u\).
  • On the chain this was \(\beta_t(k) = p(y_{t+1:T} \mid z_t = k)\). Same object, read on a tree.

The collect pass

Run the rule once per non-root node, children before parents. At the root nothing is left to send, so sum instead:

\[ p(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{z_r} p(z_r) \, \underbrace{p(y_r \mid z_r) \prod_{c \,\in\, \text{children}(r)} m_{c \to r}(z_r)}_{p(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor r \rfloor} \mid z_r) \;=\; p(\mathbf{y} \mid z_r)}. \]

  • One message per edge, each a sum over \(K\) values for each of \(K\) outputs: \(O(K^2)\) per edge, \(O(nK^2)\) in all.
  • Everything below the root is everything, so the last line is the prior times the likelihood given the root state, summed over the root state.
  • This is the whole likelihood computation. For the phylogenetics half of today it is the only pass we need.

Marginals need the other direction

The posterior at an internal node \(v\) is the joint with everything except \(z_v\) summed out. Everything below \(v\) is already in hand. What is missing is everything above:

\[ p(z_v, \mathbf{y}) \;=\; \underbrace{p(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil v \rceil}, z_v)}_{\text{from the parent } u} \; \underbrace{p(y_v \mid z_v) \prod_{c \,\in\, \text{children}(v)} m_{c \to v}(z_v)}_{p(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor v \rfloor} \mid z_v), \text{ from the children of } v} \]

Write \(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil v \rceil}\) for every observation not in the subtree below \(v\). Given \(z_v\) the two sides are independent, so the joint factorizes as shown, and \(p(z_v \mid \mathbf{y})\) is this divided by \(p(\mathbf{y})\).

The second factor exists after the collect pass. The first, \(m_{u \to v}(z_v) := p(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil v \rceil}, z_v)\), does not, yet. It is \(\alpha\) from the chain: the data on the far side, jointly with the state at this end.

The downward message

“Above \(v\)” is the rest of the tree once the edge \((u, v)\) is cut. Seen from \(u\), that is three things, all functions of \(z_u\): \(u\)’s own evidence, whatever came down to \(u\) from its parent, and the subtrees of \(v\)’s siblings. Multiply them, attach the edge, and sum out \(z_u\):

\[ \boxed{\; m_{u \to v}(z_v) = \sum_{z_u} p(z_v \mid z_u) \, \underbrace{p(y_u \mid z_u)}_{\text{evidence at } u} \, \underbrace{m_{\text{pa}(u) \to u}(z_u)}_{\text{from above } u} \underbrace{\prod_{s \,\in\, \text{children}(u),\; s \neq v} m_{s \to u}(z_u)}_{\text{from the siblings of } v} \;} \]

  • At the root nothing is above, \(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil r \rceil}\) is empty, and \(p(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil r \rceil}, z_r) = p(z_r)\): the prior is what the root “hears from above”. Set \(m_{\text{pa}(r) \to r}(z_r) := p(z_r)\).
  • A node’s downward message needs its parent’s first: pre-order, the mirror image of the collect pass.

One rule

Write \(\mathcal{N}(i)\) for the neighbours of \(i\), parent and children alike. Both boxed formulas are the same statement:

\[ \boxed{\; m_{i \to j}(z_j) = \sum_{z_i} p(z_{\text{ch}} \mid z_{\text{pa}}) \, p(y_i \mid z_i) \prod_{k \in \mathcal{N}(i) \setminus j} m_{k \to i}(z_i) \;} \]

where \((\text{pa}, \text{ch})\) is the edge \(\{i, j\}\) read in the direction of its arrow, and the root’s prior counts as a message arriving from above.

  • Take everything \(i\) knows, its own evidence and every incoming message except the one from \(j\), multiply, attach the edge, sum out \(z_i\).
  • Excluding \(j\)’s message is what keeps the subtree on \(j\)’s side from being counted twice. On a tree the two sides of an edge share nothing else, so this is exactly right. On a graph with cycles it is not enough, and next week we see what happens when people run it anyway.
  • Parent or child, up or down: one rule, applied once per edge per direction.

The schedule

Pick any node as root. Collect inward, distribute outward. Each edge carries one message in each direction: \(2(n - 1)\) messages at \(O(K^2)\) apiece, and then every marginal in the model is a product of vectors.

Exercise: it really is the marginal

Throughout, \(v\) is a node and \(u = \text{pa}(v)\) is its parent, so \(m_{v \to u}\) travels up and \(m_{u \to v}\) travels down. Everything needed is the factorization of \(p(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y})\) and the separation property, used once per step.

  1. Upward. Show by induction from the leaves that \(m_{v \to u}(z_u) = p(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor v \rfloor} \mid z_u)\). The leaf case is one line; for an internal node use that, given \(z_v\), the children’s subtrees are independent of each other.
  2. Downward. Show that \(m_{u \to v}(z_v) = p(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil v \rceil}, z_v)\), by induction from the root. You will need \(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil v \rceil} = \mathbf{y}_{\lceil u \rceil} \cup \{y_u\} \cup \bigcup_{s \neq v} \mathbf{y}_{\lfloor s \rfloor}\) and that these pieces are independent given \(z_u\).
  3. Together. Conclude that \(p(y_v \mid z_v) \prod_c m_{c \to v}(z_v) \, m_{u \to v}(z_v) = p(z_v, \mathbf{y})\) at every node, so that summing it over \(z_v\) gives \(p(\mathbf{y})\) at every node, not only at the root. On the chain this was \(\sum_k \alpha_t(k) \beta_t(k) = p(y_{1:T})\) for every \(t\).
  4. Edges. Write \(p(z_u, z_v, \mathbf{y})\) for the edge from \(u\) down to \(v\) as a product of messages, the edge conditional \(p(z_v \mid z_u)\), and evidence. This is \(\xi\) on a tree.

Implementation

On the Node class

Each node carries pot, its evidence \(p(y_v \mid z_v)\) as a vector over its own states, with the prior \(p(z_r)\) multiplied in at the root, and each non-root node carries edge, the conditional \(p(z_v \mid z_{\text{pa}(v)})\) on the branch to its parent, a matrix indexed [parent state, own state]. Messages live on the node that sends them up or receives them from above.

def collect(root):
    """Post-order. Leaves v.below = p(y_v | z_v) times every message from below,
       and v.up, the message from v to its parent. Returns p(y)."""
    for v in postorder(root):
        below = v.pot.copy()
        for c in v.children:
            below = below * c.up
        v.below = below
        if v.parent is not None:
            v.up = v.edge @ below                # sum_{z_v} p(z_v | z_pa) below(z_v)
    return root.below.sum()
  • below is the boxed upward rule before the sum: evidence times the children’s messages. The matrix product does the sum over \(z_v\) and returns a vector over the parent’s states.
  • Ten lines, and the post-order traversal from Lab 4 is doing the scheduling. Nothing here is specific to phylogenies, chains, or \(K\).

def marginals(root):
    """p(z_v | y) on every node: below times down, normalized."""
    for v in preorder(root):
        b = v.below if v.parent is None else v.below * v.down
        v.marginal = b / b.sum()

marginals is given, and it is the exercise’s conclusion in two lines: \(p(\mathbf{y}_{\lfloor v \rfloor} \mid z_v)\) is below, \(p(\mathbf{y}_{\lceil v \rceil}, z_v)\) is down, and their product normalized is \(p(z_v \mid \mathbf{y})\). The next slide tells you whether your distribute is right.

Does it work?

A random directed tree model on the seven node tree, \(K = 3\): a random root distribution, a random conditional table on every edge, and an observation with a random likelihood vector at every node. Against a brute force sum over all \(3^7\) latent configurations.

    nodes[a].add_child(nodes[b])

    v.pot = rng.random(K)                                   # p(y_v | z_v), one value per state
    if v.parent is not None:
        v.edge = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(K), size=K)          # p(z_v | z_pa): rows sum to one
root = nodes[0]
root.pot = root.pot * rng.dirichlet(np.ones(K))             # p(z_r) folded into the root's evidence

lik = collect(root); distribute(root); marginals(root)

lik_bf, marg = 0.0, {v: np.zeros(K) for v in nodes}
for cfg in itertools.product(range(K), repeat=7):

    p *= np.prod([nodes[b].edge[cfg[a], cfg[b]] for a, b in TREE_EDGES])

A chain is a tree in which every node has one child. For the HMM the evidence at node \(t\) is \(p(y_t \mid z_t)\), the conditional on every edge is \(A\), and the prior at the root is \(\pi\). Build it out of Node objects and run the same code against last week’s forward-backward.

def forward_backward(logB, pi, A):
    T, K_ = logB.shape; B = np.exp(logB)
    al, c = np.zeros((T, K_)), np.zeros(T)
    a = pi * B[0]; c[0] = a.sum(); al[0] = a / c[0]
    for t in range(1, T):
        a = (al[t-1] @ A) * B[t]; c[t] = a.sum(); al[t] = a / c[t]
    be = np.zeros((T, K_)); be[-1] = 1
    for t in range(T-2, -1, -1):
        be[t] = (A @ (B[t+1] * be[t+1])) / c[t+1]
    return al * be, np.log(c).sum()

Phylogenetics

The oldest tree model in biology

  • Evolutionary biology. Which species are most closely related, and in what order did they diverge?
  • Molecular epidemiology. SARS-CoV-2 lineages, influenza strain selection, tracing an outbreak to its source. A phylogeny of viral genomes is a transmission history.
  • Cancer genomics. Subclones are related by descent; the clonal tree is the order in which driver mutations were acquired.
  • Immunology. B cell receptor lineages in a germinal centre are a phylogeny of somatic hypermutation.

In every case the ancestors are unobserved. That is what makes it a latent variable problem, and its inference algorithm, published in 1981, is exactly the collect pass we just derived.

  • The alignment you simulated in Lab 4: one row per taxon, one column per site, and three ancestral rows we now pretend not to have.
  • Given the tree, sites are independent, so the likelihood is a product over columns and everything can be developed for one column at a time.
  • Real alignments have \(10^3\) to \(10^6\) columns. The leaves are data; the tree and the ancestors are the unknowns.

The tree is two unknowns

  • Topology: who is related to whom. Discrete, and the number of them is enormous.
  • Branch lengths \(t_e\): expected substitutions per site along each branch. Continuous, one per edge.
  • Both must usually be inferred. The ancestral nucleotides at the internal nodes are latent variables, \(K = 4\), one per node per site.
  • Rooted or unrooted? Most substitution models are time reversible, so the likelihood does not depend on where the root is placed. We root for interpretation and for the traversals, not for the arithmetic.

Substitution along a branch

A site evolves along a branch as a continuous time Markov chain on \(\{A, C, G, T\}\) with rate matrix \(Q\), so the transition matrix for a branch of length \(t\) is \(P(t) = e^{Qt}\), with

\[ P_{ab}(t) = p(\text{descendant} = b \mid \text{ancestor} = a). \]

  • \(P(s)P(t) = P(s + t)\): the model is consistent however a branch is subdivided.
  • Under Jukes-Cantor, \(P(t)\) is the closed form you used to simulate. K80 separates transitions from transversions; GTR has six exchangeabilities and four base frequencies; a Gamma distributed rate per site lets some columns evolve faster than others.
  • None of that changes what follows. The algorithm needs only a matrix \(P(t)\) per branch and a root distribution \(\pi\); swap the model and the inference code is untouched.

The likelihood of one site

\[ p(y_A, y_B, y_C, y_D \mid \tau, t) = \sum_{z_{\text{root}}} \sum_{z_{i_1}} \sum_{z_{i_2}} \pi_{z_{\text{root}}} \prod_{v \neq \text{root}} P_{z_{\text{pa}(v)} z_v}(t_v). \]

  • Three internal nodes, \(4^3 = 64\) terms. Fifty taxa gives \(4^{49} \approx 3 \times 10^{29}\), per site, with thousands of sites, repeated for every candidate tree.
  • It is the tree model with \(p(z_{\text{root}}) = \pi\), nothing observed at the internal nodes, \(p(y_v \mid z_v) = \mathbb{1}[z_v = y_v]\) at the leaves, and \(p(z_v \mid z_{\text{pa}(v)}) = P(t_v)\).
  • So we already know the way out.

Felsenstein’s pruning algorithm

Define the conditional likelihood of the subtree below \(v\), given that \(v\) is in state \(s\):

\[ L_v(s) = p\big(\text{leaves below } v \mid z_v = s\big). \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \textbf{leaf:} \quad & L_v(s) = \mathbb{1}[s = y_v] \\ \textbf{internal:} \quad & L_v(s) = \prod_{c \,\in\, \text{children}(v)} \Big( \sum_{s'} P_{s s'}(t_c) \, L_c(s') \Big) \\ \textbf{root:} \quad & p(\mathbf{y}) = \sum_s \pi_s \, L_{\text{root}}(s). \end{aligned} \]

One post-order traversal. \(O(nK^2)\) instead of \(O(K^n)\).

v.below = \(p(y_v \mid z_v) \prod_c m_{c \to v}\) conditional likelihood \(L_v\)
message c.up = \(P(t_c) \, L_c\) the bracket \(\sum_{s'} P_{ss'}(t_c) L_c(s')\)
evidence \(p(y_v \mid z_v)\) indicator at a leaf, \(\mathbb{1}\) at an internal node
conditional \(p(z_v \mid z_{\text{pa}(v)})\) transition matrix \(P(t_v)\)
\(p(\mathbf{y})\) at the root \(\sum_s \pi_s L_{\text{root}}(s)\)

Felsenstein published this in 1981; Pearl’s belief propagation came in 1982. Two communities derived the same algorithm within a year of each other, and it took a decade for anyone to notice.

Summary

What we have

question pass cost
\(p(\mathbf{y})\) for a tree, a topology, a site collect \(O(nK^2)\)
\(p(z_v \mid \mathbf{y})\) at every node collect, then distribute \(O(nK^2)\)
the most probable joint ancestral assignment max-product \(O(nK^2)\)
  • The model’s assumption, that the two sides of any edge are independent given the variable between them, is what lets every sum be pushed inward and done once. The tree is how we draw that assumption.
  • One rule, \(m_{i \to j} = \sum_{z_i} p(z_{\text{ch}} \mid z_{\text{pa}}) \, p(y_i \mid z_i) \prod_{k \neq j} m_{k \to i}\), run post-order then pre-order on the data structure you built.
  • Forward-backward and Felsenstein pruning are the same rule on a line and on a phylogeny.

Next week

  • Our rule assumed every factor sits on a node or an edge. A pedigree factor links mother, father and child; a parity check in an error correcting code constrains four bits at once. Neither is an edge.
  • Factor graphs make the factorization explicit and allow factors of any arity. The sum-product algorithm on a factor graph has forward-backward, pruning and today’s belief propagation as special cases.
  • And an example where the graph is emphatically not a tree, and people run the algorithm anyway.
