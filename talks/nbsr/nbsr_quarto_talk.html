Part 1 · From biological questions to statistical models

What is biostatistics?

Biostatistics develops statistical models and methods for learning about biological and health systems from imperfect measurements.

\[ \text{scientific question} \rightarrow \text{measurement process} \rightarrow \text{statistical model} \rightarrow \text{inference} \]

Today’s theme: the measurement process determines the estimand. Sequencing measures compositions, and that single fact reshapes every downstream multivariate question.

Biological systems come in many forms

Molecular systems

  • genes and transcripts
  • messenger RNA
  • microRNAs
  • proteins and metabolites

Microbial ecosystems

  • microbial taxa
  • microbial genes
  • community functions
  • host–microbiome interactions

Different biological systems can produce remarkably similar statistical measurement problems.

What changes in disease?

Disease changes biological systems, not isolated variables.

  • Which genes, microRNAs, microbial taxa, or pathways change?
  • Are these isolated changes, or coordinated biological programs?
  • Which changes are direct, and which are induced by changes elsewhere in the system?

The last question is the one compositional data makes hard: a change in one feature appears as a change in all of them.

From biological abundance to RNA-seq counts

Tissue/cells \(\Rightarrow\) RNA extraction \(\Rightarrow\) library preparation \(\Rightarrow\) sequencing \(\Rightarrow\) read counts.

Small RNA-seq (miRNA) is our first application; 16S microbiome sequencing is the second. Both produce a \(K\)-vector of counts per sample whose total is set by the sequencer, not by the biology.

Ways to measure molecules and microbes

Assay What is measured Main question
Bulk mRNA-seq mRNA pooled across many cells Which genes change?
Single-cell RNA-seq RNA from individual cells Which cell types and states?
Small RNA-seq miRNAs and other short RNAs Which small RNAs change?
16S / ITS sequencing Marker genes Which microbes are present?
Shotgun metagenomics Microbial DNA Which organisms and genes?
Metatranscriptomics Microbial RNA Which microbial genes are active?

Different biology, the same statistical measurement structure: a count vector \(y_i\in\mathbb{N}^K\) whose total \(n_i=\sum_j y_{ij}\) is fixed by the experiment.

Case study: restoring a disrupted microbial ecosystem

Optional — 1 minute. Fecal microbiota transplant restores a community, not a single taxon. Motivates thinking about the system as a whole.

A shared measurement structure

\[a_{ij}=\text{underlying (absolute) abundance of feature }j\text{ in sample } i.\]

But we do not directly observe \(a_{ij}\).

\[ a_{ij} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \pi_{ij}=\frac{a_{ij}}{\sum_k a_{ik}} \quad\longrightarrow\quad y_{ij} \]

Absolute abundance

\(a_i=(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{iK})\), \(a_{ij}\ge0\), \(a_i\neq\mathbf 0\)

Composition

\(\pi_i=\mathcal{C}(a_i)\): \(\pi_{ij}\ge0\), \(\sum_j\pi_{ij}=1\)

Observed counts

\(y_i=(y_{i1},\ldots,y_{iK})\), \(\sum_j y_{ij}=n_i\)

\(\mathcal{C}(a)=a/\mathbf 1^\top a\) is the closure operator. It is scale invariant: \(\mathcal{C}(ta)=\mathcal{C}(a)\) for every \(t>0\).

Illustration: the sequencer cannot see the total

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Sample 1: } & a_1 = (10,80,10), \\ \text{Sample 2: } & a_2 = (20,160,20). \end{aligned} \]

Sample 2 contains twice as many molecules of every feature, but both samples have the same composition \((0.1,0.8,0.1)\).

Sequenced to depth \(n=1000\), both give \(\mathbb{E}[y] = (100,800,100)\).

Note

Sequencing depth is set by library preparation, pooling, and budget — not by the number of molecules in the sample. The total \(A_i=\sum_k a_{ik}\) is not identified from the counts.

Absolute and relative effects differ

For conditions \(g\in\{0,1\}\), the scientific target is the absolute log-fold change \(\delta_j=\log\{a_j(1)/a_j(0)\}\). The counts inform only the relative change:

\[ \underbrace{ \log \frac{\pi_j(1)}{\pi_j(0)} }_{r_j\ (\text{relative})} = \underbrace{ \log \frac{a_j(1)}{a_j(0)} }_{\delta_j\ (\text{absolute})} + \underbrace{ \left\{-\log \frac{A(1)}{A(0)}\right\} }_{c_{\text{rel}}\ (\text{common to all } j)}. \]

Treatment doubles feature A only; every other feature appears to decrease by the common shift \(c_{\rm rel}=-\log(60/50)=-0.18\).

This one picture is the whole problem. Every method in this space is, at some level, a strategy for estimating that orange dashed line.

Part 2 · Compositional data analysis

The simplex as a sample space

A composition lives on the simplex

\[ \mathbb{S}^K=\Big\{\pi\in\mathbb{R}^K:\ \pi_j>0,\ \textstyle\sum_{j}\pi_j=1\Big\}, \]

a \((K-1)\)-dimensional space embedded in \(\mathbb{R}^K\). Compositional data analysis works in this open simplex; a truly absent feature (\(a_{ij}=0\)) puts \(\pi_i\) on the boundary, where log-ratios are undefined.

Why ordinary multivariate tools mislead

  1. Euclidean distance ignores relative change.
  2. The sum constraint forces the covariance.

Related issues (backup slides): the arithmetic mean is not the natural centre; closure can induce positive spurious correlation; results change when some features are dropped and the rest rescaled to sum to 1 (subcompositional incoherence).

Two pitfalls, one slide each, then log-ratios as the fix. The others are in the backup slides if a question comes up.

Pitfall 1 · Euclidean distance ignores relative change

pair (\(K=2\)) change in feature 1 Euclidean Aitchison \(\|\operatorname{CLR}x-\operatorname{CLR}y\|\)
\((0.01,\,0.99)\) vs \((0.02,\,0.98)\) doubled 0.0141 0.4973
\((0.49,\,0.51)\) vs \((0.50,\,0.50)\) +2% 0.0141 0.0282

A doubling and a 2% change are the same Euclidean distance apart. The Aitchison distance, built on log-ratios, measures relative change and separates them.

Ask the class which pair is the “bigger” change biologically before revealing the takeaway. Note: once data are proportions, scale is already gone by closure; the real requirement is sensitivity to relative change (and, next slide, invariance to perturbation). Euclidean distance on raw counts additionally has a literal scale problem — it depends on sequencing depth.

Pitfall 1 · Distances should not depend on the protocol

Perturbation \(x\oplus p=\mathcal{C}(x_1p_1,\ldots,x_Kp_K)\): multiply component-wise, then divide by the sum to return to the simplex.

It models a feature-specific bias applied to every sample, e.g. feature 1 captured twice as efficiently: \(p=(2,1)\).

Apply it to both compositions of the first pair:

composition \(x\) composition \(y\) Euclidean Aitchison
before \((0.01,\,0.99)\) \((0.02,\,0.98)\) 0.0141 0.4973
after \(\oplus\,p\) \((0.02,\,0.99)/1.01=(0.0198,\,0.9801)\) \((0.04,\,0.98)/1.02=(0.0392,\,0.9607)\) 0.0274 0.4973

The Euclidean distance nearly doubles because of the protocol; the Aitchison distance is unchanged, since \(\operatorname{CLR}(x\oplus p)=\operatorname{CLR}(x)+\operatorname{CLR}(p)\) and the common \(\operatorname{CLR}(p)\) cancels in the difference.

Feature-specific capture, amplification, and mapping biases act on sequencing data exactly as a common perturbation. A Euclidean analysis partly measures the protocol; a log-ratio analysis cancels it. Perturbed values are truncated to 4 decimals (exact: 0.980198…, 0.960784…), which is why each perturbed composition shows a sum of 0.9999.

Pitfall 2 · Closure forces the covariances to balance

Randomness is across samples; within every sample \(\sum_j\pi_{ij}=1\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^K\operatorname{Cov}(\pi_j,\pi_k) &\ \underset{\text{bilinearity}}{=}\ \operatorname{Cov}\Big(\textstyle\sum_j\pi_j,\ \pi_k\Big) \ \underset{\sum_j\pi_j\,\equiv\,1}{=}\ \operatorname{Cov}(1,\pi_k)=0\\[4pt] \Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{j\neq k}\operatorname{Cov}(\pi_j,\pi_k) &\ =\ -\operatorname{Var}(\pi_k)\qquad(\text{split off the } j=k \text{ term}). \end{aligned} \]

  • Matrix form: \(\Sigma_\pi\mathbf 1=\mathbf 0\), so \(\Sigma_\pi\) is singular.
  • Strength: with equal variances, the average correlation is \(\bar\rho=-1/(K-1)\); perfectly negative when \(K=2\), whatever the biology.
  • Intuition: proportions are a fixed budget. If one feature is above its average, the others must be below theirs by the same total amount.

Dirichlet example. \(\operatorname{Cov}(\pi_j,\pi_k)=-\bar\pi_j\bar\pi_k/(1+\kappa)\): every covariance is negative and set by one concentration \(\kappa\), a very rigid dependence model.

Step by step: (1) covariance is bilinear, so the sum comes out; (2) the total is the constant 1 in every sample and a constant has zero covariance with anything; (3) the j=k term is Var(π_k), move it across. ρ̄ = −1/(K−1) follows from 0 = Var(Σ_j π_j) = Kσ² + K(K−1)ρ̄σ². Ask: “If independent gamma abundances are closed, what do you get?” Answer: Dirichlet. Independence on the absolute scale still produces negative correlation on the simplex.

Aitchison’s answer: work with log-ratios

A log-ratio is invariant to scaling, \(\log\{(ta_j)/(ta_k)\}=\log(a_j/a_k)\); hence \(\log(\pi_j/\pi_k)=\log(a_j/a_k)\). It measures relative change, cancels common perturbations, and is unchanged when other features are dropped.

Two ways to turn a composition into log-ratios. Example: \(\pi=(0.2,\ 0.3,\ 0.5)\).

ALR: compare to a reference feature

\[ \operatorname{ALR}(\pi)_j=\log\frac{\pi_j}{\pi_K},\quad j=1,\ldots,K-1 \]

Reference = feature 3: \(\big(\log\tfrac{0.2}{0.5},\ \log\tfrac{0.3}{0.5}\big)=(-0.9162,\ -0.5108)\)

\(K-1\) unconstrained numbers; depends on the reference.

CLR: compare to the geometric mean

\[ \operatorname{CLR}(\pi)_j=\log\frac{\pi_j}{g(\pi)},\quad g(\pi)=\Big(\prod_k\pi_k\Big)^{1/K} \]

\(g=(0.2\cdot0.3\cdot0.5)^{1/3}=0.3107\): \((-0.4405,\ -0.0351,\ 0.4757)\)

\(K\) numbers that sum to zero; no reference needed. \(\log g(\pi)\) is the mean of \(\log\pi_k\), so CLR just centres the logs.

In log-ratio coordinates, ordinary tools (means, distances, PCA, regression) behave sensibly. The Aitchison distance is just Euclidean distance on CLR vectors.

Both transforms will reappear in Part 3: we fit in ALR coordinates and report in CLR coordinates. ILR coordinates and the formal Aitchison geometry are in the backup slides.

Distributions on the simplex

Dirichlet \(\pi\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha)\)

  • closure of independent \(\text{Gamma}(\alpha_j,1)\)
  • one concentration \(\kappa=\sum_j\alpha_j\)
  • covariance: always negative, no structure

Logistic-normal (Aitchison & Shen, 1980)

  • \(\operatorname{ALR}(\pi)\sim N_{K-1}(\mu,\Sigma)\), equivalently \(\pi=\operatorname{softmax}(\xi)\), \(\xi\sim N_K\)
  • arbitrary covariance structure among log-ratios
  • no closed-form moments on the simplex

In practice we combine them with a count layer:

\[ \underbrace{\xi_i\sim N(m,\Sigma)}_{\text{dependence}}\ \to\ \underbrace{p_i\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\kappa\,\operatorname{softmax}(\xi_i))}_{\text{extra variation}}\ \to\ \underbrace{y_i\sim\operatorname{Mult}(n_i,p_i)}_{\text{sampling}} . \]

This Dirichlet–multinomial logistic-normal (DM-LN) model is our simulation engine later.

Transform, then regress: strengths and limitations

Log-ratio methods (LinDA, ANCOM-BC2, ALDEx2) are widely used for good reasons: they respect compositional geometry, are fast, and plug into familiar regression tools.

Some features of sequencing counts are harder to handle once the counts are transformed:

  • zeros: log-ratios are undefined at zero, so a pseudocount is needed and results can depend on it;
  • finite depth: the same observed proportion is far more precise at \(n_i=10^7\) than at \(10^3\);
  • heteroskedasticity: variance depends on abundance and depth;
  • feature-specific overdispersion: some features are inherently more variable;
  • mechanism: the regression model is separate from the process that generated the counts.

Can we model counts directly while respecting compositional geometry?

Part 3 · Negative binomial softmax regression

The NBSR model

Samples \(i=1,\ldots,M\); features \(j=1,\ldots,K\); covariates \(x_i\in\mathbb{R}^P\).

\[ \begin{aligned} y_{ij}\mid \pi_{ij},\phi_{ij} &\sim \operatorname{NB}\big(n_i\,\pi_{ij},\ \phi_{ij}\big),\\ \pi_i &= \operatorname{softmax}(\eta_i),\qquad \eta_{ij}=x_i^\top\beta_j, \end{aligned} \]

with \(\mu_{ij}=n_i\pi_{ij}\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(y_{ij})=\mu_{ij}+\mu_{ij}^2/\phi_{ij}\) (\(\phi\) is a precision).

NB likelihood

On the count scale: zeros need no pseudocount.

Softmax mean

\(\pi_i\in\mathbb{S}^K\); features coupled through the normaliser.

Feature-wise precision

Each feature (and condition, sample) has its own overdispersion.

Note

The likelihood is a product of NB densities. It does not constrain \(\sum_j y_{ij}=n_i\) for each draw — only \(\sum_j\mathbb{E}[y_{ij}]=n_i\). A deliberate relaxation that buys feature-specific overdispersion.

Softmax is the inverse CLR

Let \(\bar\eta_i=K^{-1}\sum_j\eta_{ij}\) and \(\tilde\eta_i=\eta_i-\bar\eta_i\mathbf 1\). Then

\[ \operatorname{softmax}(\eta_i)=\operatorname{CLR}^{-1}(\tilde\eta_i), \qquad \operatorname{CLR}(\pi_i)_j=\eta_{ij}-\bar\eta_i . \]

NBSR is a count-scale log-ratio regression. The identifiable part of the linear predictor is the CLR of the fitted composition — but we never take a log of an observed count.

Adding the same constant to every \(\eta_{ij}\) leaves \(\pi_i\) unchanged: the composition cannot see that direction. This is the same \(\mathbf 1\) direction as the singular covariance in Pitfall 2, and it is why the coefficients need a constraint (next slide).

If asked: the softmax Jacobian (matrix of first derivatives) is ∂π/∂ηᵀ = diag(π) − ππᵀ, with entries π_j(1{j=k} − π_k). Multiplying by 1 gives π − π(πᵀ1) = 0, so 1 is in its null space: rank K−1, the formal version of “a common shift in η doesn’t move π”.

Identifiability: two coordinate systems for one model

\(\operatorname{softmax}(\eta+c\mathbf 1)=\operatorname{softmax}(\eta)\), so \(\beta_j\mapsto\beta_j+\gamma\) for all \(j\) leaves the likelihood unchanged. We need \(P\) constraints.

ALR constraint \(\beta_K=0\)

  • \(K-1\) free coefficient vectors
  • standard multinomial-logit parameterisation
  • convenient for optimisation & sampling

CLR constraint \(\sum_j\beta_j=0\)

  • symmetric in features
  • \(\eta_i\) lies in zero-sum plane \(\Rightarrow\eta_i=\operatorname{CLR}(\pi_i)\)
  • reference-free reporting

They parameterise the same mean model: \(\beta^{\rm CLR}_j=\beta^{\rm ALR}_j-\bar\beta^{\rm ALR}\), \(\ \bar\beta^{\rm ALR}=\frac1K\sum_k\beta_k^{\rm ALR}\).

Fit in ALR, report in CLR, applying the map to every posterior draw.

For a binary condition in column \(p\) of \(x_i\), the reference-free contrast is

\[ \Delta^{\rm clr}_j=\operatorname{CLR}(\pi(1))_j-\operatorname{CLR}(\pi(0))_j=\beta_{jp}-\tfrac1K\textstyle\sum_k\beta_{kp}, \]

the change of feature \(j\) relative to the geometric mean of the composition — linear in \(\beta\).

Subtle point worth raising for this audience: ALR and CLR are equivalent as likelihoods but not as Bayesian models under independent priors — an iid N(0, σ²) prior on ALR coefficients induces a slightly tighter prior on the reference feature’s CLR coefficient. The asymmetry is O(1/K).

Part 4 · Scale: abundance-dependent dispersion

What the precision φ measures

  • \(p_i=\mathcal{C}(a_i)\): the realised composition of sample \(i\) (the composition from Part 1). It varies between biological replicates: \(p_i\sim F\).
  • \(\pi_i=\mathbb{E}[p_i]\): the expected composition given the covariates. This is what NBSR models, \(\pi_i=\operatorname{softmax}(x_i^\top\beta)\).

Sequencing then counts molecules from \(p_i\): \(\ y_{ij}\mid p_{ij}\sim\text{Poisson}(n_ip_{ij})\). By the law of total variance,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(y_{ij}) =\underbrace{n_i\pi_{ij}}_{\text{sequencing noise}} +\underbrace{n_i^2\operatorname{Var}(p_{ij})}_{\text{biological variation}} . \]

Match to an NB with mean \(\mu_{ij}=n_i\pi_{ij}\) and variance \(\mu_{ij}+\mu_{ij}^2/\phi_{ij}\):

\[ \frac{1}{\phi_{ij}}=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(p_{ij})}{\pi_{ij}^2}=\operatorname{CV}^2(p_{ij}). \]

\(1/\phi\) = between-sample variability of the realised composition (small \(\phi\) = variable feature).

Equivalently CV²(y) = 1/μ (technical) + 1/φ (biological), the “biological coefficient of variation” of McCarthy et al. (2012). Gamma mixing gives the NB exactly; for other F it is a two-moment match.

The Dirichlet case motivates the model

If \(p_i\sim\operatorname{Dir}(\kappa\pi_i)\), then \(\operatorname{CV}^2(p_{j})=\dfrac{1-\pi_j}{\pi_j(1+\kappa)}\), so

\[ \boxed{\ \log\phi_j=\operatorname{logit}(\pi_j)+\log(1+\kappa)\ } \]

Precision is linear in logit-abundance with unit slope. Rare features are intrinsically noisier on the relative scale.

A single concentration \(\kappa\) ties every feature to the same curve. Real data need more flexibility (next slide).

The NBSR dispersion model

NBSR generalises the Dirichlet relationship:

\[ \log\phi_{ij}=b_0+b_j+b_\pi\operatorname{logit}(\pi_{ij})+w_i^\top b_w . \]

term role Dirichlet value
\(b_0\) global concentration \(\log(1+\kappa)\)
\(b_j\) feature-specific departure (hierarchical, \(b_j\sim N(0,\sigma_b^2)\)) \(0\)
\(b_\pi\) mean–dispersion slope, prior \(N(1,0.1^2)\) \(1\)
\(w_i^\top b_w\) sample or batch effects, e.g. one offset \(c_i\) per sample \(\log(1+\kappa_i)-\overline{\log(1+\kappa)}\)

Because \(\pi_{ij}\) depends on the covariates, precision automatically differs between conditions as well as between features.

Setting b_pi = 0 and w = 0 recovers DESeq2-style per-feature dispersion.

Real data: dispersion depends on condition

If precision differs across conditions but is modelled as constant, standard errors are misestimated and intervals are miscalibrated.

Why the coupling regularises sparse features

Fixed precision. For an observed zero, \(\ell(0;\mu,\phi)=-\phi\log(1+\mu/\phi)\), so

\[ \frac{\partial\ell(0)}{\partial\log\mu}=-\frac{\mu\phi}{\mu+\phi}\ \approx\ -\phi\qquad(\mu\gg\phi). \]

Raising \(\log\mu\) by \(\Delta\) costs only \(\approx\phi\Delta\) per zero. With small \(\phi\), many zeros barely resist a few large counts.

Abundance-dependent precision. For rare features \(\operatorname{logit}\pi\approx\log\pi\), so with \(b_\pi\approx1\), \(\phi=c\mu\) with \(c\) fixed. Then

\[ \ell(0;\mu,c\mu)=-c\mu\log\!\Big(1+\frac1c\Big), \qquad \text{loss from }\mu_0\to\mu_0e^\Delta = \phi_0\,(e^{\Delta}-1)\log\!\Big(1+\frac{\mu_0}{\phi_0}\Big). \]

The penalty grows exponentially in \(\Delta\) instead of linearly.

Example: \(\phi=0.05\), \(\mu_0=1\), \(m=10\) zeros, \(\Delta=4\): 1.97 nats (fixed) vs 81.6 nats (coupled).

The model controls sparse features through its likelihood, rather than through a pseudocount, a prevalence filter, or a pseudocount-sensitivity filter.

Regularisation in a benchmark replicate

Three null miRNAs, 10 control samples. Top: control-group log-likelihood vs. assumed proportion. Bottom: posteriors under an oracle fit (true dispersion, held fixed) and NBSR. Dashed: truth; dotted: posterior means.

Striking point: NBSR beats the oracle that knows the true dispersion for the rarest miRNA. The oracle’s likelihood is nearly flat to the right, so a couple of large counts drag its posterior up by ~2 log units. NBSR’s coupled precision gives the zeros teeth. The abundant miRNA (right) is unaffected.

How NBSR compares with other count models

Model Couples features? Overdispersion Zeros
Multinomial logistic yes (softmax) none — \(\operatorname{Var}\) fixed by mean fine
Dirichlet–multinomial yes one \(\kappa\) shared by all features fine

Part 5 · The estimand: the compositional shift

From CLR effects to absolute effects

Centring removes the unobserved total — but leaves a different common term:

\[ \Delta^{\rm clr}_j = r_j-\frac1K\sum_k r_k =(\delta_j+c_{\rm rel})-\frac1K\sum_k(\delta_k+c_{\rm rel}) =\delta_j+\underbrace{(-\bar\delta)}_{c_{\rm CLR}} . \]

Definition. The CLR compositional shift is the scalar \(c_{\rm CLR}\) with \(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j=\delta_j+c_{\rm CLR}\) for all \(j\).

The data identify \(\Delta^{\rm clr}\), but not \(\delta\): adding the same constant \(t\) to every \(\delta_j\) also raises \(\bar\delta\) by \(t\), so \(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j=\delta_j-\bar\delta\) is unchanged. Every \(\delta+t\mathbf 1\) fits equally well.

Biologically, that is multiplying every abundance by \(e^t\), a change in the total the sequencer cannot see (Part 1).

To break it we need either

  • external scale information (spike-ins, flow cytometry, qPCR of total load), or
  • a structural assumption about \(\delta\).

(Optional) How spike-ins fix the scale

Add the same known amount of a synthetic molecule to every sample before library prep. Its true abundance does not change between conditions.

Treatment doubles A; B, C unchanged; spike-in S = 10 in both groups.

A B C S
control 10 10 10 10
treated 20 10 10 10
log fold change A B
relative (composition incl. S) 0.4700 −0.2231
ratio to spike-in 0.6931 0
truth \(\delta\) 0.6931 0

The spike-in is a reference whose absolute level is known not to move, so log-ratios against it recover \(\delta\) directly: it pins down the free constant \(t\).

Caveats: \(\delta\) is “per unit the spike-in was added to” (per cell ≠ per µg RNA), and the spike-in must face the same capture and amplification biases as real features.

Relative: the sequenced composition includes S, so π_A = 10/40 → 20/50 and log(0.4/0.25) = log 1.6 = 0.4700; π_B = 10/40 → 10/50, log 0.8 = −0.2231. Ratio to spike-in: log{(y_A/y_S) treated / (y_A/y_S) control} = log(2/1) = log 2. Caveats: the spike-in must be added per the right unit (per cell vs per µg RNA gives a different δ), and it must go through the same capture and amplification as the real features. Flow cytometry or qPCR of total load work similarly, by measuring the total A_i directly.

The modal-null assumption

Following LinDA (Zhou et al., 2022): the across-feature distribution of absolute effects has a unique mode at zero,

\[ \operatorname{mode}_{1\le k\le K}(\delta_k)=0 . \]

Exact sparsity is sufficient but not necessary: unchanged features may scatter near zero, so long as they remain the densest group.

Because the mode is translation-equivariant,

\[ \operatorname{mode}_k(\Delta^{\rm clr}_k)=\operatorname{mode}_k(\delta_k)+c_{\rm CLR}=c_{\rm CLR}, \]

giving the estimator

\[ \Delta^{\rm adj}_j=\Delta^{\rm clr}_j-\widehat m,\qquad \widehat m=\operatorname{mode}_k\big(\Delta^{\rm clr}_k\big)\ \ \text{(Gaussian KDE)}. \]

Compare: ANCOM-BC estimates the null centre of a 3-component Gaussian mixture by EM; ALDEx2 reports \(\Delta^{\rm clr}\) and does not correct.

What happens to the variance?

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\Delta^{\rm adj}_j) =\operatorname{Var}(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j) +\operatorname{Var}(\widehat m) -2\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j,\widehat m). \]

  • \(\widehat m\) is a non-smooth functional of the whole \(K\)-vector, correlated with each component it corrects. No tidy delta method.
  • LinDA argues the last two terms vanish as \(K,n\to\infty\); our earlier NBSR paper also treated \(\widehat m\) as fixed.

Is that safe? It depends on which features you ask about.

Our solution: recompute \(\widehat m\) inside every posterior draw. Both missing terms are then propagated automatically.

Discussion question for the class: for which features would you expect Cov(Δclr_j, m̂) to be large? Answer: very abundant, precisely estimated features — they have small own-variance, so the shift’s variance is comparable, and they influence the mode estimate. We’ll see this in the variance decomposition table.

Part 6 · Inference

Priors and the per-draw estimand

Priors. \(\beta_{jp}\sim N(0,\sigma_{\beta p}^2)\) under \(\beta_K=0\); \(b_0\sim N(0,1)\); \(b_j\sim N(0,\sigma_b^2)\), \(\sigma_b\sim N^+(0,0.5^2)\); \(b_\pi\sim N(1,0.1^2)\).

Empirical Bayes option for real data: fit with \(\sigma_{\beta p}=10\), then set \(\sigma_{\beta p}\) by matching a zero-centred normal’s upper tail to the precision-weighted 0.95 quantile of \(|\hat\beta_{jp}|\) (as in DESeq2), and refit.

Per-draw estimand. For each posterior draw \(s\):

\[ \beta^{(s)}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{centre}\ }\ \Delta^{{\rm clr},(s)}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{KDE mode}\ }\ \widehat m^{(s)}\ \longrightarrow\ \Delta^{{\rm adj},(s)}_j=\Delta^{{\rm clr},(s)}_j-\widehat m^{(s)} . \]

The posterior of \(\Delta^{\rm adj}_j\) is the empirical distribution of \(\{\Delta^{{\rm adj},(s)}_j\}_s\).

Gold standard: HMC (Stan, NUTS), with clean convergence diagnostics. But: 50 / 132 / 345 min per fit at \(n=10/20/40\).

Laplace approximation with Gaussian draws

  1. Optimise: \(\hat\beta=\arg\max_\beta\log p(\beta\mid Y)\) (dispersion parameters at their mode).
  2. Hessian: \(-H=-\nabla^2_\beta\log p(\beta\mid Y)\big|_{\hat\beta}\), dimension \((K-1)P\).
  3. Cholesky, never invert: \(-H=LL^\top\).

Draw from the Gaussian with one triangular solve per draw:

\[ \beta^{(s)}=\hat\beta+L^{-\top}z^{(s)},\qquad z^{(s)}\sim N(0,I)\ \Rightarrow\ \beta^{(s)}\sim N\big(\hat\beta,(-H)^{-1}\big). \]

The same factor gives the delta-method variance of the (linear) CLR contrast exactly:

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j)=q_j^\top(-H)^{-1}q_j=\|L^{-1}q_j\|^2,\qquad q_{j,(k,d)}=\mathbf 1\{d=p\}\big(\mathbf 1\{k=j\}-\tfrac1K\big). \]

Gradients and Hessian in matrix form in PyTorch: a fit takes ~2 minutes on one core vs. hours for HMC.

Remaining approximations: Gaussian shape; dispersion held at its mode. The shift is treated exactly as in the sampler.

The Hessian is dense because the softmax couples all features: ∂²/∂β_j∂β_k ≠ 0 for j≠k. For K≈400, P=2 that’s ~800×800 — trivial. For K in the tens of thousands (genes) one would need structure (block-diagonal + low-rank, Woodbury) — a nice open computational problem.

Three decision rules on the same posterior

Rule Statistic Uses
Local false sign rate \(\operatorname{lfsr}_j=\min(p^+_j,1-p^+_j)\), \(p^+_j=\frac1S\sum_s\mathbf 1\{\Delta^{{\rm adj},(s)}_j>0\}\) full posterior shape
Posterior-\(z\) + BH \(z^{\rm post}_j=\overline{\Delta^{\rm adj}_j}/\operatorname{sd}(\Delta^{\rm adj}_j)\) first two moments, shift uncertainty included
Delta method + BH (earlier paper) \(z^{\rm DM}_j=\widehat{\Delta}^{\rm adj}_j/\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j)^{1/2}\) shift treated as fixed

If the marginal posterior is Gaussian, \(p^{\rm post}_j=2\,\operatorname{lfsr}_j\).

Important

Under a continuous prior, there are no exact nulls in the posterior. A sign probability ranks features well, but a threshold on it is not a frequentist error rate.

Part 7 · Simulation design and results

Simulation design: DM-LN with exact ground truth

For sample \(i\) in condition \(g_i\), with unnormalised abundance vector \(\alpha(g)\) and \(\kappa(g)=\sum_j\alpha_j(g)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \xi_i&\sim N\big(m(g_i),\Sigma\big), & \theta_i&=\operatorname{softmax}(\xi_i),\\ p_i&\sim\operatorname{Dir}\big(\kappa(g_i)\,\theta_i\big), & Y_i&\sim\operatorname{Mult}(n_i,p_i), \end{aligned} \qquad m_j(g)=\log\alpha_j(g)-\tfrac12\Sigma_{jj}. \]

Effects imposed on absolute abundance, \(\alpha_j(1)=\alpha_j(0)e^{\delta_j}\), so \(\delta_j\), \(c_{\rm rel}=-\log\{\kappa(1)/\kappa(0)\}\) and \(r_j\) are all known exactly.

Nesting. \(\Sigma=0\): Dirichlet–multinomial. \(\kappa\to\infty\): multinomial logistic-normal (fido/pibble; LinDA’s log-normal simulation). Conditional on \(\xi_i\): same count model as the Poisson-lognormal of ANCOM-BC2.

None of the fitted models — including NBSR — contains \(\Sigma\). The microbiome benchmark tests every method under misspecification.

Two calibrated benchmarks

miRNA (McCall et al., 2017)

  • CD4\(^+\) T cells, 596 miRNAs → ~400 after filtering
  • reads concentrated in a few features
  • \(\Sigma=0\) (Dirichlet–multinomial)
  • LOW / MED / HIGH: perturb 20 / 10 / 5 features by baseline abundance
  • depth \(\approx10^7\); \(n\in\{10,20,40\}\) per group

Microbiome (Baxter et al., 2016)

  • 172 healthy stool samples, 249 genera
  • diffuse, sparse; median depth 9,000

  • ALL25, ALL50 (all up), BAL50 (25 up / 25 down)

Why FDR can rise with \(n\)

In ALL50 the CLR shift is about \(-0.21\):

  • ALDEx2 tests \(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j=0\) with no correction → null genera sit at \(-0.21\) at every \(n\).
  • LinDA’s mode correction leaves a smaller residual bias.

Important

A bias that stays fixed while the standard error shrinks like \(n^{-1/2}\) turns into false discoveries as \(n\) grows. More data makes a biased test worse.

NBSR’s null estimates average near zero at every \(n\).

Per-sample precision offsets: a multivariate trick

\[ \log\phi_{ij}=b_0+b_j+b_\pi\operatorname{logit}(\pi_{ij})+c_i,\qquad \textstyle\sum_i c_i=0 . \]

  • \(n\) extra parameters, but each \(c_i\) is informed by all \(K\) counts of sample \(i\) → cheaply identified.
  • \(c_i\) does not enter the mean, so it cannot absorb the compositional shift; it changes how much each sample is trusted (NB weight \(\propto(\mu+\mu^2/\phi)^{-1}\)).

Two sources of gain:

  1. Between-group concentration. When genera increase, \(\kappa(1)>\kappa(0)\); a single \(b_0\) cannot represent both. Centred sample indicators span the centred treatment indicator, so the \(c_i\) absorb \(\log\{\kappa(1)/\kappa(0)\}\).
  2. Within-group heterogeneity. Fitted \(c_i\) track each sample’s share of zeros → down-weighting of poorly fit samples.

Summary of evidence

Methodology

  • NBSR = count-scale log-ratio regression (softmax \(=\operatorname{CLR}^{-1}\))
  • abundance-dependent precision regularises sparse, zero-heavy features through the likelihood
  • shift recomputed per draw → its variance and covariance propagate
  • Cholesky-Laplace with Gaussian draws: minutes, not hours

Empirical

  • miRNA: highest power at controlled FDR, lowest RMSE at every \(n\)
  • Laplace reproduces HMC rankings; slightly narrow at \(n=10\)
  • microbiome (misspecified): highest power at matched FDR, lowest RMSE
  • SELEX: no mechanism-contradicting calls

Part 8 · Shape and dependence: open problems

Latent factors on the simplex

\[ \eta_{ij}=x_i^\top\beta_j+z_i^\top\gamma_j,\qquad z_i\in\mathbb{R}^q,\ q\ll K . \]

In CLR coordinates, \(\operatorname{Cov}(\operatorname{CLR}\pi_i\mid x_i)\approx \tilde\Gamma\tilde\Gamma^\top\) with \(\tilde\Gamma=(I-\tfrac1K\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top)\Gamma\): a factor model on the Aitchison simplex, fitted on the count scale.

Captures shared residual variation from cell-type mix, tumour purity, immune infiltration, batch, unmeasured subtypes.

Why it matters here: the microbiome benchmark’s covariance \(\Sigma=LL^\top+\operatorname{diag}(d)\) is exactly what every current method — NBSR included — ignores.

Classical factor-analysis issues all reappear: rotational non-identifiability of (z, γ), choosing q, and posterior multimodality — plus the compositional 1-direction. Questions for the class: how would you identify Γ? What does the softmax do to the interpretation of a loading?

Further open problems

  • The modal-null assumption. When does it fail (large coordinated programs, a dominant feature changing)? How should external scale information (spike-ins, total load) enter?
  • Exact nulls and Bayesian FDR. A continuous prior gives no posterior mass at \(\delta_j=0\); spike-and-slab or regularised-horseshoe priors around the unknown shift rather than around the ALR reference.
  • Scalable computation. \((K-1)P\)-dimensional dense Hessians for \(K\sim 2\times10^4\) genes; block + low-rank structure; variational alternatives.
  • Beyond two groups. Longitudinal and spatial compositions, single-cell pseudo-bulk, multimodal integration.
  • Causal reading of differential abundance under compositional measurement.

Backup: ILR coordinates and Aitchison geometry

Transform Definition Lives in Properties
ALR \(\log(\pi_j/\pi_K)\), \(j<K\) \(\mathbb{R}^{K-1}\) unconstrained, but reference-dependent
CLR \(\log(\pi_j/g(\pi))\) zero-sum hyperplane in \(\mathbb{R}^K\) symmetric; covariance singular
ILR \(V^\top\log\pi\), \(V^\top V=I_{K-1}\), \(V^\top\mathbf 1=0\) \(\mathbb{R}^{K-1}\) orthonormal; basis-dependent

Aitchison geometry. Perturbation \(\pi\oplus\nu=\mathcal{C}(\pi_1\nu_1,\ldots,\pi_K\nu_K)\), powering \(t\odot\pi=\mathcal{C}(\pi_1^t,\ldots,\pi_K^t)\), and inner product \(\langle\pi,\nu\rangle_A=\langle\operatorname{CLR}\pi,\operatorname{CLR}\nu\rangle\) make \(\mathbb{S}^K\) a \((K-1)\)-dimensional Euclidean vector space; CLR and ILR are isometries.

\(VV^\top = I_K-\tfrac1K\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^\top\): CLR is the ILR coordinates expressed back in \(\mathbb{R}^K\).

Backup: the arithmetic mean is not the centre

The natural centre of a set of compositions is the closed geometric mean:

\[ \operatorname{cen}(\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_n)=\mathcal{C}\Big(\exp\Big\{\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\log\pi_{i1}\Big\},\ \ldots\Big), \]

the arithmetic mean in log-ratio coordinates, mapped back.

Example. Average \((0.01,\,0.99)\) and \((0.0001,\,0.9999)\):

  • arithmetic: first feature \(=0.005\), pulled toward the larger value;
  • geometric centre: first feature \(\approx0.001\), the midpoint on the log-ratio scale (each is \(10\times\) away).

Curvature. A straight line in log-ratio space is a curve on the simplex. The arithmetic mean of a curved cloud can fall off the data.

Consequence for PCA: on raw proportions, abundant features dominate the total variance and curvature wastes components → use CLR-PCA (Aitchison, 1983).

Backup: spurious correlation can have either sign

Absolute abundances \(a=(a_1,\,a_2,\,X)\): \(a_1,a_2\) nearly constant and independent; \(X\) a highly variable “bloom” feature. Then

\[ \pi_1=\frac{a_1}{a_1+a_2+X},\qquad \pi_2=\frac{a_2}{a_1+a_2+X} \]

share a random denominator, so \(\pi_1\) and \(\pi_2\) rise and fall together.

Same 300 simulated samples, three answers. Left: absolute abundances. Middle: proportions in the full composition. Right: after dropping feature 3 and re-closing.

Pearson (1897): ratios with a common denominator are correlated. “Negative bias” describes the average, not any particular pair.

Middle panel: correlation +0.85 from nothing but the shared denominator. The right panel is the subcompositional-incoherence example (later backup slide). Real-data analogue: a single dominant taxon (e.g. a Bacteroides or Proteobacteria bloom) makes all the minor taxa look positively co-abundant.

Backup: the variation matrix

Aitchison’s variation matrix

\[ T_{jk}=\operatorname{Var}\Big\{\log\frac{\pi_j}{\pi_k}\Big\}. \]

  • unaffected by closure: \(\log(\pi_j/\pi_k)=\log(a_j/a_k)\);
  • \(T_{jk}=0\) ⟺ features \(j\) and \(k\) are proportional across samples;
  • unchanged when features are dropped and re-closed (subcompositional coherence).

Consequences for multivariate practice

  • Correlation networks on proportions are unreliable → SparCC, SPIEC-EASI, proportionality (\(\rho_p\)) were built to address this.
  • Methods needing an invertible covariance (Gaussian graphical models, Mahalanobis distance, LDA) fail on \(\Sigma_\pi\). CLR does not fully fix this: \(\operatorname{Cov}(\operatorname{CLR}\pi)\mathbf 1=\mathbf 0\) too. Work in ILR coordinates, or with \(T\).
  • Dependence models must be specified on log-ratios — the motivation for the logistic-normal, and for latent factors later in the talk.

Relation between T and the CLR covariance Γ: T_jk = Γ_jj + Γ_kk − 2Γ_jk, the same identity that links a distance matrix to a Gram matrix in classical MDS. Good link to the multivariate course.

Backup: subcompositional incoherence

A subcomposition keeps features \(S\subset\{1,\ldots,K\}\) and re-closes: \(\pi_S=\mathcal{C}(\pi_j:\ j\in S)\).

A method is subcompositionally coherent if its conclusions about the features in \(S\) do not depend on whether the other features were measured.

analysis \(\operatorname{corr}(\cdot_1,\cdot_2)\)

full composition \(\{1,2,3\}\) \(+0.85\)
subcomposition \(\{1,2\}\) \(-1.00\)

quantity coherent?
correlation / covariance of proportions ✗
Euclidean distance, arithmetic mean ✗

Backup: every sequencing dataset is a subcomposition

What decides which features are in the panel?

  • Assay: small RNA-seq captures short RNAs only; 16S captures only taxa matching the primers.
  • Annotation: which miRNAs are in MirGeneDB; which genera the taxonomy resolves.
  • Filtering: prevalence filters, minimum-count filters, dropping unannotated reads.

An incoherent analysis gives answers that depend on these arbitrary choices.

An honest caveat for NBSR. The CLR contrast \(\Delta^{\rm clr}_j\) is measured against the geometric mean of the panel, so it is not coherent: drop features and it moves. This is one reason we target the absolute effect \(\delta_j\) instead:

  • \(\delta_j=\log\{a_j(1)/a_j(0)\}\) is a property of feature \(j\) alone;
  • the mode-based shift correction recovers it approximately coherently, provided the unchanged features remain the mode of the retained panel.

Could a prevalence filter that mostly removes null features threaten the modal-null assumption?

Answer sketch: yes in principle. If filtering preferentially removes nulls (rare features are mostly null), the null mode becomes less dominant relative to perturbed features. In practice the null group usually remains the largest, but this is exactly why NBSR avoids prevalence filtering and handles rare features through the dispersion model instead.

Backup: negative binomial parameterisation

\[ y\sim\operatorname{NB}(\mu,\phi):\quad p(y)=\frac{\Gamma(y+\phi)}{\Gamma(\phi)\,y!}\Big(\frac{\phi}{\mu+\phi}\Big)^{\phi}\Big(\frac{\mu}{\mu+\phi}\Big)^{y}, \]

\[ \mathbb{E}y=\mu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}y=\mu+\mu^2/\phi,\qquad I(\log\mu)=\frac{\mu\phi}{\mu+\phi}=\Big(\frac1\mu+\frac1\phi\Big)^{-1}. \]

The Fisher information on the log scale is capped by \(\min(\mu,\phi)\): deeper sequencing cannot average away between-sample variability.

Backup: ALR → CLR gradient

With \(\beta=(\beta_1^\top,\ldots,\beta_{K-1}^\top)^\top\) and \(\beta_K=0\),

\[ \Delta^{\rm clr}_j= \begin{cases} \beta_{jp}-\frac1K\sum_{r<K}\beta_{rp}, & j<K,\\ -\frac1K\sum_{r<K}\beta_{rp}, & j=K, \end{cases} \qquad q_{j,(k,d)}=\mathbf 1\{d=p\}\Big(\mathbf 1\{k=j\}-\frac1K\Big). \]

Linear in \(\beta\), so the delta-method variance is exact within the Gaussian approximation.
